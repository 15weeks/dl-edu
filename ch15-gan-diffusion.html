GAN & Diffusion

Generative models

Two ways to learn a data distribution and draw new samples from it: GANs, where a forger (generator) and an inspector (discriminator) compete, and diffusion, which adds noise to pictures a little at a time and learns to remove it in reverse. Then AI ethics and how to keep learning after this.

2GANs: the generator–discriminator game

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

Can a model learn to imitate a distribution without ever writing it down as a formula?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

GAN
generative adversarial network. Two networks that learn by competing (Goodfellow et al. 2014).
G (generator)
The forger: takes noise z and makes a fake sample.
D (discriminator)
The inspector: outputs the probability that a sample is real.

In words: D learns to output 1 for real and 0 for fake; G learns to make D output 1 for its fakes too. One step each, in turns. At the end G's distribution = the data distribution and D(x) = 0.5 (it cannot tell).

real data

RESULTS: 1-D GAN, G (1-16-16-1), D (1-32-32-1), 3000 steps (PyTorch)

→ Meaning. With one peak, after 3000 steps D is almost flat at 0.50–0.56 where the data is: it can no longer tell. Two peaks are harder: at step 500 the mean leaned to one side (−0.39), and by step 3000 both peaks were covered, though the shapes are lumpy. It did not happen here, but a G that makes only one peak and stops (mode collapse) is a common GAN failure.

One line to remember. GAN = G tries to fool, D tries to catch. Through the contest G's distribution comes to resemble the data's.

→ Meaning. The linear GAN still has speckle noise in the background after 20 epochs: an FFN generator does not know that neighbouring pixels should be alike. At epoch 5 many look-alike digits (9s and 3s) appear, a hint of mode collapse. The CNN GAN draws smooth strokes and a good mix of digits by epoch 10. One line to remember. Image GANs use transpose convs in G (chapter 11, section 4) and convs in D. Training is unstable, so tricks such as Adam β₁ 0.5, LeakyReLU and BatchNorm are needed.

4The idea of diffusion: add noise, then undo it

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

Turning a picture into noise is easy. What if a network could learn the reverse, turning noise into a picture?

  • √ᾱ_t the picture's share (1 → 0)
  • √(1−ᾱ_t) the noise's share (0 → 1)
  • ᾱ_t a number computed from β_t in section 5; here we borrow section 5's values
  • ε noise drawn from a standard normal, the same size as the picture

WORKED EXAMPLE: one white pixel x₀ = 1, drawn noise ε = −0.5

Borrow section 5's values ᾱ₁₀₀ = 0.897 and ᾱ₅₀₀ = 0.0786 and compute at t = 100 and t = 500.

→ Meaning. At t = 100 the original value 1 still shows as 0.79, but at t = 500 the result is −0.20 and the noise dominates. The two shares squared always add to 1 (0.947² + 0.321² = 1): only the mixing ratio changes.

One line to remember. Diffusion = picture → noise by a fixed formula; noise → picture by a network, one step at a time.

5The forward process and the noise schedule

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

How much noise is added per step? How much is left after 1000 steps?

WORKED EXAMPLE: T = 1000, β rising linearly from 0.0001 to 0.02 (DDPM, Ho et al. 2020)

→ Meaning. At t = 500 the picture's share is only √0.0786 = 0.28; at 1000 it is 0.0064, practically pure noise. Because the products stack up, one can jump straight to x_t (the formula of section 4): training never has to walk all 1000 steps.

One line to remember. The per-step noise β_t is small; the accumulated ᾱ_t goes from 1 to 0. x_t can be made from x₀ in one jump.

6The denoising network and its training (DDPM loss)

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

What does the network learn to predict?

In words: pick any picture x₀ and any time t, mix in noise ε to make x_t. The network ε_θ looks at x_t and t and guesses "what noise was mixed in". The same idea as chapter 10's denoising autoencoder, done for every noise level.

WORKED EXAMPLE: a 2-pixel picture x₀ = (1, −1), t = 500 (√ᾱ = 0.280, √(1−ᾱ) = 0.960)

Draw noise ε = (0.3, −1.2) and mix it in.

Say the network looks at x₅₀₀ and t = 500 and guesses the noise as ε_θ = (0.5, −1.0). Square the difference in each pixel.

→ Meaning. The network predicts the mixed-in noise, not x₀. The formula's ‖·‖² is a sum over pixels, while F.mse_loss in the code below is the mean, so with 2 pixels it is half: a different size, the same thing being minimised.
t = torch.randint(0, T, (B,))                 # any time
eps = torch.randn_like(x0)                    # noise to mix in
xt = abar[t].sqrt() * x0 + (1 - abar[t]).sqrt() * eps
loss = F.mse_loss(unet(xt, t, c), eps)        # guess the noise
loss.backward(); opt.step()

This chapter's U-Net: 575,169 parameters, 60,000 MNIST images, 12 epochs (Apple GPU). t goes in as a sine vector, the same as chapter 14's positional encoding.

One line to remember. DDPM training = regression (MSE) on the mixed-in noise ε. The structure is a U-Net that shrinks and grows.

7Sampling: from noise to an image

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

With a trained ε_θ, how is a new picture made?

  • α_t = 1 − β_t, the share kept in one step (α without the bar; ᾱ_t is α multiplied from 1 to t)
  • ε_θ(x_t, t) the noise the network guesses
  • z fresh noise drawn at each step, 𝒩(0, I)

In words: start from pure noise; 1000 times, subtract a little of the noise the network guesses and add a little fresh noise (z). The last step adds no z.

WORKED EXAMPLE: take section 4's pixel one step back from t = 500

x₅₀₀ = −0.1996 (section 4's −0.200), β₅₀₀ = 0.01004, α₅₀₀ = 1 − 0.01004 = 0.98996 and √(1 − ᾱ₅₀₀) = 0.9599. Say the network guessed the noise exactly, ε_θ = −0.5, and the fresh noise drawn is z = 0.1. First the factor on the noise to subtract:

Put everything into the formula.

→ Meaning. One step changes the value by only about 0.01. Repeating such small steps from t = 500 down to 1 removes the noise bit by bit and brings the value back towards a picture. If the network's guesses are off, those errors also build up a little at every step.

One line to remember. Sampling = start from noise and step back 1000 times with ε_θ. Slower than a GAN's single pass, but stable and varied.

8Conditioning, guidance and latent diffusion (Stable Diffusion)

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

How do we get it to draw what we ask for, like "a 7" or "a photo of a cat"?

  • c condition (a digit, text)
  • w guidance scale: push the difference between with and without the condition w times further
  • ∅ no condition (the class was dropped 10% of the time in training)

WORKED EXAMPLE: one pixel, the guess is 0.6 with condition c = "7" and 0.2 without

→ Meaning. The change the condition makes (0.6 − 0.2 = 0.4) is added w more times: 0.6 + w × 0.4. The larger w, the more "seven-ness" is exaggerated. As the results below show, the samples become more accurate but more alike.
guidance w

RESULTS: 50 samples per digit, share read as the requested digit (judged by a small CNN)

→ Meaning. With the condition but no guidance (w = 0), 90% are right, but mistakes such as a 5 drawn like a 6 or a 9 slip in. Raising w makes almost all of them right, while the strokes get thicker and the samples more alike: variety is traded for accuracy. The judging CNN itself has 98.0% test accuracy, so these shares carry 1–2% of error.
Latent diffusion (Stable Diffusion v1): diffusion in the latent space of an autoencoder (chapter 10) instead of on the picture

One line to remember. Feed a condition c along, and use guidance w to follow it more strongly. For large pictures, diffuse in a latent space to save compute.
