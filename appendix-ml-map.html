DL-EDU · APPENDIX A

ML map

Machine-learning keywords outside deep learning

A map splits machine learning into seven stages. This appendix collects the keywords from that map that the DL-Edu chapters (3–15) do not cover. Each keyword gets a one-line meaning, a key formula and a small numeric example, and each stage gets at least one figure that trains in the browser.

1Classic ML: splitting with lines, neighbours and probabilities

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

Points can be split into two groups without a neural network. What does each method use to draw its boundary?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

supervised learning
Learning x → y from data that carry the correct label y. Chapters 3–9 are all supervised learning.
SVM
Support vector machine. Picks the boundary with the widest gap (margin) between the two groups.
k-NN
k-nearest neighbours. A majority vote of the k closest points.
NB
Naive Bayes. Bayes' rule with the "naive" assumption that features are independent.
KeywordIn one lineKey formulaLink to DL-Edu
supervised learningLearn x → y from examples with answersmin Σ L(f(xᵢ), yᵢ)All of chapters 3–9
logistic regressionA linear score turned into a probability by a sigmoidp = σ(w·x + b)The same as one neuron in chapter 3
SVMThe widest road between the groups; only points near the boundary (support vectors) set itmax 2/‖w‖, yᵢ(w·xᵢ + b) ≥ 1Hinge loss; kernels give curved boundaries
k-NNMajority vote of the k nearest points; no training stepŷ = majority(N_k(x))Small k overfits (chapter 5)
Naive BayesMultiply each feature's probability per class and compareP(y | x) ∝ P(y) Π P(xⱼ | y)The classic spam filter
  • w direction normal to the boundary (shorter = wider margin)
  • y class (spam / not spam)

In words: an SVM "lays the widest road between the two groups". Naive Bayes compares "how likely these words are in spam × how common spam is" with the same product for normal mail.

Example: spam detection with Naive Bayes

40% of mail is spam. The word "free" appears in 50% of spam and 5% of normal mail. What is the chance that a mail containing "free" is spam?

→ Meaning. One word moves the chance from 40% to 87%. With more words, each word's probability is multiplied in (that is why independence is assumed).

SVM: with w = (1, 1) and b = −3, the point (2, 2) gives w·x + b = 1, so it sits on the margin: a support vector. The road width is 2/‖w‖ = 2/√2 = 1.414.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. Blobs: all four methods reach similar test accuracy. One straight line is enough for this data.
  2. Moons: logistic regression and the SVM draw straight lines and Naive Bayes draws an ellipse, so they stop at 88–89% test accuracy. Only k-NN follows the curved boundary and reaches 100%.
  3. k-NN on the blobs with k = 1: 100% on training but 84% on test, with a ragged boundary (overfitting). With k = 7 the test accuracy is 92%. For the SVM, the black rings are the support vectors.
method
data

One line to remember. Logistic regression = a probability, SVM = the widest road, k-NN = a neighbour vote, Naive Bayes = multiplied probabilities. The biggest difference is whether the boundary is straight or can bend.

2Trees & Ensembles: twenty questions, and crowds of them

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

What if the data are split by asking one question at a time, such as "is x₁ < 0.3?" And why does a collection of such trees do better?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

CART
Classification and regression tree. The most common way to build a decision tree.
Gini
Gini impurity. How mixed one node is; 0 when it holds a single class.
bagging
Bootstrap aggregating. Build many models on resampled data (with replacement) and average them.
boosting
Add weak models one after another; each fixes where the previous ones were wrong.
XGBoost
eXtreme Gradient Boosting. A fast gradient-boosting library that uses second derivatives and regularization.
KeywordIn one lineKey formulaNotes
decision treeSplit one cell at a time with axis-parallel questionsthe question that lowers Gini = 1 − Σ p_k² the mostEasy to read; deep trees overfit
random forestMany trees on resampled data with random features, averagedp = (1/B) Σ p_bBagging; reduces the wobble (variance)
AdaBoostRaise the weight of misclassified points so the next model focuses thereα = ½ ln((1 − ε)/ε)The first boosting method (1995)
gradient boostingKeep adding small trees fitted to the remaining error (= −gradient)F_m = F_{m−1} + η h_mGradient descent in function space (chapters 2, 7)
XGBoostGradient boosting + second derivatives + a penalty on tree sizeObj = Σ L + Σ (γT + ½λ‖w‖²)The workhorse for tabular data
  • p_k share of class k in a node
  • η how much is added each round (learning rate)
  • h_m the m-th small tree (learns the remaining error)

In words: a tree picks the question that lowers the mixing (Gini) the most. Boosting lets the next tree learn "how wrong the current model is" and adds a little of it.

Example ①: the score of one question (Gini)

→ Meaning. The tree computes this drop for every possible question (axis and cut position) and takes the largest. Then it repeats inside each new cell.

Example ②: AdaBoost weights

→ Meaning. The 20% that were wrong get twice the weight and the 80% that were right get half. After rescaling to a total of 1, the wrong points hold exactly 50% of the weight, so the next model must pay attention to them.

Example ③: one round of gradient boosting

→ Meaning. The first model is a single mean. The next tree (a stump) learns "lower for the first two, higher for the third", and only η = 0.5 of it is added. This is repeated hundreds of times.

THINGS TO TRY: (a) one tree and a forest

  1. Decision tree, depth 1 → 3 → 10: the boundary splits into finer steps. On the starting data the training accuracy climbs from 87.5% to 100%, but the test accuracy stops at 87.5% from depth 5 on.
  2. Random forest, depth 10 (20 trees): the boundary becomes smoother and the test accuracy rises to 90%. Change the number of trees from 1 to 60.
model

THINGS TO TRY: (b) gradient boosting

  1. Round 0 → 1 → 5: steps appear one by one on top of the single mean. The red lines (remaining error) get shorter.
  2. η 1.0: fits within a few rounds but also follows the noise. η 0.1: comes down slowly. That is why a small η with many rounds is used in practice.
η

One line to remember. Tree = a chain of questions that lower the mixing most. Forest = the average of many trees (less wobble). Boosting = the next tree learns the remaining error and a little of it is added.

3Clustering: grouping without answers

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

Given points without labels, how do we group nearby points together? And who decides how many groups there are?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

unsupervised learning
Finding structure in data without labels. The chapter 10 autoencoder belongs here too.
DBSCAN
Density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise. Grows groups along dense regions.
ε, minPts
DBSCAN's neighbourhood radius, and the number of neighbours a core point needs.
linkage
How the distance between two groups is measured: single (closest pair), complete (farthest pair), average (mean).
KeywordIn one lineKey formulaNotes
k-meansRepeat "assign to the nearest centre" ↔ "move each centre to its mean"J = Σ ‖x − μ_c(x)‖²k is fixed in advance; suits round groups
k-means++Pick starting centres spread far apartP(x) ∝ D(x)²Fewer bad starts
hierarchicalKeep merging the two closest groups into a tree (dendrogram)single / complete / average linkageThe cut height picks the number of groups
DBSCANGroups spread from core points that have at least minPts neighbours within ε|N_ε(x)| ≥ minPtsAny shape; marks noise separately
  • μ_k centre of group k
  • c(i) the group of point i
  • J sum of squared distances from points to their centres

In words: both (1) assigning and (2) moving lower J or leave it unchanged, so the loop always stops. There is no guarantee that where it stops is the best answer; it depends on the start.

Example: k-means in 1-D (points 1, 2, 3, 9, 10, 11, starting centres 1 and 4)

→ Meaning. The point 3 first joins the starting centre 4, then returns to the left group once the centres move. In step 3 no assignment changes, so the loop ends.

THINGS TO TRY: (a) k-means and DBSCAN

  1. Blobs + k-means: press "One step" several times and watch J fall. Restart with "New data" a few times: sometimes one centre swallows two blobs. With k-means++ this is rare.
  2. Moons + k-means (k = 2): the two moons are cut in half by a straight line. DBSCAN (ε 0.35–0.5) finds the two moons as they are.
  3. DBSCAN with a small ε (0.2): many small groups and lots of noise (grey). From 0.6 up: the two moons join into one group.
method
data

THINGS TO TRY: (b) hierarchical clustering

  1. Lower the cut height (red dashed line) and the number of groups grows: one group when cut at the top, one per point at the bottom.
  2. Single linkage only looks at the closest pair, so its merge heights are low. Complete linkage looks at the farthest pair, so they are high.
linkage

One line to remember. k-means = k centres and repeated mean moves (k-means++ = a good start). Hierarchical = a tree of merges cut at the height you want. DBSCAN = spreads along dense regions, and loners are noise.

4Compress & Detect: shrinking to see, finding odd points

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

To look at 10-dimensional data we must shrink it to 2 dimensions. What do we keep? And how do we find points that are "not like the rest" without labels?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

PCA
Principal component analysis. Projects onto the directions of largest variance (principal components, PCs).
t-SNE
t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding. Lays data out in 2-D while keeping close neighbours close.
KL
Kullback–Leibler divergence. How different two probability distributions are (the partner of cross-entropy in chapter 1).
perplexity
In t-SNE, the rough number of points treated as neighbours.
KeywordIn one lineKey formulaNotes
PCAProject onto the direction of largest variance (linear)Σv = λv, share kept λ₁ / ΣλFast and reversible; the linear version of the autoencoder (chapter 10)
t-SNEA 2-D picture that keeps neighbours (non-linear)min KL(P ‖ Q), q ∝ (1 + ‖yᵢ − yⱼ‖²)⁻¹For looking only; do not trust distances between groups or group sizes
isolation forestRandom cuts isolate odd points quicklys = 2^(−E[h] / c(n))Anomaly detection without labels
autoencoderShrink and rebuild; a large rebuild error means an anomalychapter 10DL-Edu chapter 10
  • v principal-component direction (PC)
  • y position in the 2-D picture
  • E[h] mean number of cuts until a point is alone

In words: PCA picks "the direction that casts the longest shadow". t-SNE keeps "points that were close stay close in 2-D". An isolation forest measures "how many cuts until a point stands alone".

Example ①: PCA of a 2×2 covariance

→ Meaning. Pressed onto one line at 45°, 75% of the variance is kept. Going from 2-D to 1-D keeps three quarters of the information. Figure (a) below uses this data.

Example ②: isolation-forest score (256 samples)

→ Meaning. A point alone after 3 cuts on average scores 0.82 (anomalous); a point that takes 10 cuts, like an ordinary one, scores 0.51 (ordinary). c(n) is the mean number of cuts an ordinary point needs among n.

THINGS TO TRY: (a) PCA

  1. Direction 0° → 45°: the shadows on the blue line (blue dots) spread the most. The variance kept peaks at about 75%.
  2. 135°: the narrowest (about 25%). That is the second PC. "Find PC1" shows the exact angle.

THINGS TO TRY: (b) PCA and t-SNE

  1. "Run t-SNE": the points start bunched together and then split into groups. Groups that overlap in the PCA panel come apart in t-SNE.
  2. Perplexity 5 → 30: a small value breaks the data into small clumps; a large one looks more at the overall shape.
perplexity

THINGS TO TRY: (c) isolation forest

  1. Click a point on the outside: a few black lines (random cuts) leave it alone. A point in the middle needs many more. "Outside ↔ middle" switches between the two.
  2. Background colour = score; the more orange, the more anomalous. "Another tree" changes the cut lines, but the mean score barely changes.

One line to remember. PCA = a linear projection onto large-variance directions. t-SNE = a non-linear picture that keeps neighbours (for looking). Isolation forest = points that are alone quickly are anomalies.

5Reinforcement Learning: learning from rewards

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

With no right answers, only a reward saying "good (+)" or "bad (−)", can we learn what to do in each situation?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

RL
Reinforcement learning. An agent acts in an environment, receives rewards and learns from them.
MDP
Markov decision process. A problem defined by states s, actions a, rewards r and next-state probabilities.
Q(s, a)
Action value. The expected total reward after taking a in state s and acting well afterwards.
SARSA
State, Action, Reward, next State, next Action: the five values used in the update.
DQN
Deep Q-network. A neural network computes Q instead of a table (Atari, 2015).
ε-greedy
With probability ε take a random action (explore), otherwise the best one.
KeywordIn one lineKey formulaNotes
MDPThe frame for writing down an RL problem(S, A, P, R, γ)γ = discount on future rewards
Q-learningUpdate Q with the best value of the next state (off-policy)Q ← Q + α[r + γ max Q(s′, ·) − Q]Learns the optimal path regardless of exploration
SARSAUpdate with the value of the next action actually taken (on-policy)Q ← Q + α[r + γ Q(s′, a′) − Q]Accounts for the risk of exploring, so it is cautious
DQNQ as a neural network + replay buffer + target networkL = (r + γ max Q_target − Q_θ)²The networks of chapters 8 and 11 used for Q
policy gradientPush the policy π(a | s) directly uphill on reward∇J = E[∇ log π(a | s) · G]Continuous actions, stochastic policies
actor-criticActor = policy, critic = value V; subtracting a baseline cuts the wobbleA = r + γV(s′) − V(s)A2C, PPO; RLHF for LLMs belongs to this family
  • α update size
  • r this reward
  • γ discount on future rewards (0–1)
  • A advantage: how much better than expected

In words: inside the brackets is "what just happened (r + value of the next state) − what I expected". Q is corrected by α times that miss (the TD error). Policy gradient raises the probability of actions that went better than expected.

Example ①: one Q update

→ Meaning. From the same experience, Q-learning assumes "next time I will act best" and moves Q to 4.0; SARSA uses the next action it actually chose (possibly an exploring one) and moves Q to 0.4. This difference makes the two paths differ in the cliff figure below.

Example ②: one policy-gradient step (three arms, all at 1/3 at first)

→ Meaning. The rewarded arm's probability rises from 0.333 to 0.356. With a critic, the advantage replaces G: with r = 1, V(s) = 4, V(s′) = 5 and γ = 0.9, A = 1 + 4.5 − 4 = 1.5.

THINGS TO TRY: (a) cliff walking

  1. Press "Train 50 episodes" 6–10 times. The Q-learning path (blue line) is the shortest route right along the cliff edge (13 steps). Switch to SARSA: its path detours one or two rows higher.
  2. Lower chart: during training SARSA (orange) usually collects more reward. Q-learning walks next to the cliff and sometimes falls off (−100) because of the random ε actions.
  3. Lower ε to 0.01 and "Start over": with almost no exploration SARSA's path also moves close to the short route.
show

THINGS TO TRY: (b) policy gradient and a critic

  1. Press "Pull 100 times" a few times: the share of arm 3 (green), which has the highest mean reward, keeps growing.
  2. Switch to "critic baseline" and repeat "Start over" + 200 pulls several times: the average speed is similar, but unlucky runs become rarer. Over 20 repeats, the lowest arm-3 probability after 200 pulls rises from 0.61 to 0.69 (the means are 0.85 vs 0.84).
baseline

One line to remember. RL = learning from rewards. Q-learning updates with the best value (bold), SARSA with the action actually taken next (careful). Policy gradient raises the policy directly, and a critic's baseline reduces the wobble.

6Genetic Algorithm: finding answers by evolution

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

When we cannot take derivatives, or there are several peaks, can imitating evolution find a good answer?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

GA
Genetic algorithm. Each generation, a population of candidate answers is selected, mixed and slightly changed.
fitness
The score of one answer.
elitism
The best few are copied unchanged into the next generation.
KeywordIn one lineKey formula or rule
populationMany candidate answers (20 points in the figure)Rebuilt every generation
selectionAnswers with high scores become parentsroulette: Pᵢ = fᵢ / Σf; tournament: the best of a few
crossoverMix parts of two parents into a childone-point crossover, mixing ratio u
mutationChange the child a little at random; lets the search escape trapsbit-flip probability p_m, or + σ·noise
  • f score (fitness)
  • a, b two parents
  • σ mutation size

In words: pick parents in proportion to score (selection), mix two of them (crossover) and shake the result a little (mutation). No gradient is needed.

Example: making as many 1s as possible (OneMax, 8 bits)

→ Meaning. Cutting after the third bit and swapping the tails can produce a child that collects the good parts of both parents. An answer with score 6 becomes a parent twice as often as one with score 3.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. The first 20 candidates (violet dots) are scattered over the whole landscape. Press "One generation" a few times: the dots gather towards the peaks.
  2. "30 generations" (mutation 0.25): most runs reach the top of the highest peak (1.0, black ring), 19 times out of 20. Occasionally the population stays on the 0.7 peak at the lower left.
  3. Set mutation to 0, then "Start over" → "30 generations" several times: with mixing alone the population shrinks to one spot quickly and either misses the summit or gets stuck on a lower peak (8 times out of 20). Mutation does the exploring and the fine-tuning.

One line to remember. GA = repeated selection (good ones become parents), crossover (mixing) and mutation (shaking). No derivatives needed, and mutation lets the search escape local optima.
