Overfitting

Overfitting, cross-validation and regularization

A model that does well on its training data but badly on new data. We see why, then learn in pictures how to measure it by splitting the data (cross-validation) and how to prevent it (regularization: λ, dropout, L1 and L2).

1Overfitting and generalization

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

If the training error is close to 0, is the model good?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

overfitting
Memorising even the noise in the training data, then getting new data wrong.
underfitting
A model so simple it cannot even fit the training data.
generalization
Doing well on data the model has never seen.
  • E_train error on the training data
  • E_test error on unseen data
  • a large gap = overfitting

WORKED EXAMPLE: fitting polynomials to 15 points (200 test points)

→ Meaning. Degree 14 has 15 parameters for 15 points and passes almost exactly through them (smallest training error). But it swings wildly between the points, and the test error explodes.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. Degree 0 → 3: both errors fall together.
  2. Degree 10 and up: the red curve jumps between the points, and on the right the test error (orange) shoots up.

One line to remember. Judged by training error alone, more complex always looks better. The error on unseen data traces a U: too simple underfits, too complex overfits.

2Splitting into train, dev and test

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

Where does "unseen data" come from? What do we use to choose settings?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

dev
development set, also called the validation set. Used to choose settings.
hold-out
Data set aside and never used for training.

One line to remember. Learn on train, choose on dev, and look at test only once, at the end. Shuffle before splitting.

3k-fold cross-validation

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

What if the data are too few to set aside a big dev set? Every point takes a turn at validation.

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

CV
cross-validation.
k-fold
Split the data into k groups (folds) and train k times, validating on a different group each time.
LOO
leave-one-out. k = the number of data points: leave each one out in turn.
  • k number of folds = number of training runs
  • E_val⁽ᶠ⁾ error on fold f of the model trained without fold f

WORKED EXAMPLE: choosing the degree for section 1's 15 points with k = 5

→ Meaning. The degree chosen without ever looking at the test data (5) has test error 0.125, close to the best degree (4, 0.093). With only 15 points the choice wobbles a little with k (k = 3 picks 3).

One line to remember. k-fold = train k times and average the validation error. A way to choose settings without wasting data.

5Regularization: penalising complexity

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

Can we use a complex model and still prevent overfitting?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

regularization
Methods that add a "complexity penalty" to the loss to keep the model calm.
λ
lambda. The strength of the penalty.
ridge
Regularization that penalises the sum of squared weights (coefficients) (= L2).
  • L the original loss
  • R the penalty (e.g. Σ w²)
  • λ the balance between them

WORKED EXAMPLE: adding ridge to section 1's degree 14

→ Meaning. Still degree 14, but with the coefficients held small the test error falls to about that of degree 3 (0.095). λ itself is chosen like any setting, with dev or k-fold (sections 2–3).

One line to remember. Regularization = loss + λ × penalty. λ too small overfits; λ too large underfits.

6train() mode and eval() mode

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

Tools like dropout are only for training. How are they switched off for testing?

drop = torch.nn.Dropout(p=0.5)

7Dropout

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

Why does switching off random neurons during training reduce overfitting?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

p
The probability of switching a unit off (dropout rate).
m
The mask: 1 = on, 0 = off.

In words: every step trains a network with a different set of neurons missing. No neuron can be relied on alone, so the work gets shared. Multiplying by 1/(1 − p) keeps the average size the same.

WORKED EXAMPLE: 60 noisy points, a 2-24-24-1 network, 500 epochs

→ Meaning. Dropout deliberately lowers the training score to stop memorisation. The train–test gap shrinks from 19 to 6 points (mean of 3 seeds).

THINGS TO TRY

  1. Train both: on the left the boundary bends towards individual training points to get all of them right. On the right a few training points are missed, and the test score is higher. Dark points = training data, faint points = test data.

One line to remember. Dropout = switch neurons off with probability p during training only. The training score drops a little and the train–test gap shrinks.

8L1 and L2 weight regularization

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

What changes if the weight penalty uses |w| instead of w²?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

L1, lasso
Penalises |w₁| + |w₂| + …. Points with the same penalty form a diamond.
L2, ridge
Penalises w₁² + w₂² + …. Points with the same penalty form a circle. PyTorch's weight_decay.
  • L1's slope = λ·sign(w): the same push however small w is, all the way to 0
  • L2's slope = λ·w: the push weakens as w shrinks, so it stops near 0

WORKED EXAMPLE: the answer without a penalty is w* = (2, 0.6)

→ Meaning. L1 sets the less important w₂ to exactly 0 (from λ ≥ 0.34). That is why L1 is used to "pick the features to keep".

HOW TO READ THE FIGURE

  • Blue contours = the original loss L (lowest at w* in the middle). Green = all points with the same penalty as the current solution (circle, diamond). Orange dashes = the path the solution takes as λ grows from 0.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. Raise λ slowly: L1's solution sticks to a corner of the diamond (on the horizontal axis), so w₂ = 0. L2 shrinks both a little at a time along its circle.

One line to remember. L2 shrinks every weight evenly; L1 sets some weights exactly to 0.
