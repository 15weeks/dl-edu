Gradient descent

Derivatives and how models learn

A model "learns" by nudging its numbers, a little at a time, in the direction that makes the loss (how wrong it is) smaller. The derivative (slope) tells it which direction. This chapter leans on pictures more than words: move the figures, then check the numbers in the grey boxes.

1What it means for a model to "learn"

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

How does a computer find the line ŷ = w·x that passes closest to four points?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

parameter
A number the model adjusts as it learns. Here there is only one, w.
ŷ
"y hat": the model's prediction. y is the correct answer.
loss, L
How wrong the model is, as a single number. Smaller is better.
MSE
mean squared error. A loss that squares each error and takes the mean.
  • w the number we adjust
  • w·xᵢ prediction ŷ
  • yᵢ correct answer
  • n number of points (4)

In words: square the gap between prediction and answer, then take the mean.

WORKED EXAMPLE: the loss at w = 1, then one step

→ Meaning. A slope of −14.85 says "making w larger lowers the loss". One step takes the loss from 7.38 to 2.25. After 10 steps w = 1.99, the best value.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. Press One step a few times: the line on the left moves onto the points and the ball on the right rolls to the bottom.
  2. Set the w slider to 3: the red errors flip to the other side and the slope turns positive.

One line to remember. Learning = repeat predict → loss → slope → update until the loss is small.

2Derivatives: the slope graph

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

If we measure the slope at every point of a curve, what do we get?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

f′(x)
derivative. The slope at x. Read it "f prime of x".
h
A very small change in x.
  • f′(x) slope = rate of change for a tiny change
  • n the power comes down in front and drops by one

Examples: x² → 2x, x³ → 3x², x³ − 3x → 3x² − 3 (differentiate each added term on its own).

WORKED EXAMPLE: the slope of x³ at x = 2

→ Meaning. Near x = 2, a small increase in x makes x³ grow 12 times as much. The rule and the direct measurement agree.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. Draw the slope graph: the tangent on top moves and draws the graph below.
  2. On x³ − 3x, set x = ±1: the tangent is flat and the graph below reads 0.
f(x)

One line to remember. The derivative is a new graph made of the slope at every point. For polynomials, xⁿ → n·xⁿ⁻¹.

3Finding the bottom with derivatives

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

How does the derivative show where the loss is smallest (the bottom)?

  • green f′ < 0, downhill
  • red f′ > 0, uphill
  • where downhill turns into uphill = a bottom

WORKED EXAMPLE: the bottom of f(x) = x² − 4x + 5

→ Meaning. x = 2 is the bottom: on either side f(1.9) = f(2.1) = 1.01, higher. But when the formula is complicated we cannot solve f′ = 0 by hand. That is why the next sections walk down a little at a time.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. x³ − 3x: f′ = 0 in two places. One is a bottom, the other a top.
  2. x⁴/4 − x²: two bottoms. This comes back in section 7.
f(x)

One line to remember. At a bottom the slope is 0, with downhill (−) on its left and uphill (+) on its right.

4The product rule and the chain rule

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

How do we differentiate a product of functions, or a function inside a function? A neural network is many functions stacked inside each other, so the chain rule is the key.

Product rule

In words: when a rectangle u × v grows, the new area = the right strip (v·Δu) + the top strip (u·Δv). The corner is so small it vanishes.

WORKED EXAMPLE: y = x²·(x + 1) at x = 2

→ Meaning. Multiplying out y = x³ + x² and differentiating gives 3·4 + 2·2 = 16 too. Same answer.

A function inside a function (chain rule)

In words: when x changes a little, u changes du/dx times as much, and y changes dy/du times that. The stretch factors multiply.

WORKED EXAMPLE: y = (3x + 1)² at x = 1 (u = 3x + 1 = 4)

→ Meaning. If x grows by 0.1, u grows by 0.3 and y by about 8 × 0.3 = 2.4. The three rulers below show this. Backprop in chapter 3 does this multiplication layer by layer.

HOW TO READ THE FIGURE

  • All three rulers have the same tick spacing. The shaded cell = a small change. Going down, the cell gets several times wider.

One line to remember. The derivative of nested functions = the product of each layer's stretch factor (the chain rule).

5Gradient descent: one step against the slope

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

When we cannot solve f′ = 0, how do we find the bottom? Look at the slope and walk down a little at a time.

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

GD
gradient descent. Taking small steps against the slope.
η, LR
eta, the learning rate. The number that sets the size of each step.
  • x where we are now
  • η step size
  • f′(x) slope here
  • − go the opposite way to the slope

WORKED EXAMPLE: f(x) = (x − 2)² + 1, x = 5, η = 0.1

→ Meaning. The slope is positive → move left. Near the bottom the slope gets smaller, so the steps shrink by themselves: 4.4, 3.92, 3.54, 3.23, …

THINGS TO TRY

  1. Press One step a few times: the steps get shorter (because the slope gets smaller).
  2. Drag the ball to the left of the bottom (x = 0): the slope is negative → it moves right.

One line to remember. x ← x − η·f′(x). Big slope, big step; small slope, small step.

6The learning rate: step size

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

What happens when η is too small or too large?

In words: every step multiplies the distance to the bottom (x = 2) by (1 − 2η). That one factor decides between four outcomes.

ηfactor 1 − 2ηWhat happensx after 10 steps
0.020.96barely shrinks: slow3.99
0.20.6shrinks steadily: about right2.02
0.9−0.8flips sign as it shrinks: zigzag2.32
1.05−1.1keeps growing: divergence9.78

A LEARNING RATE THAT SHRINKS (dynamic learning rate)

→ Meaning. Take big steps first to move fast, then small steps to settle at the bottom. Even η₀ = 1.05, which diverged before, arrives at x = 2.00 after 30 steps.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. η = 0.1 and 0.9 end up in the same place after 10 steps (2.32), because 0.8¹⁰ = (−0.8)¹⁰.
  2. Set η = 1.05, then switch to Shrinking: the divergence stops.

One line to remember. Small η is slow, large η zigzags, larger still diverges. Big steps first and small steps later is the usual fix.

7Local minima and saddle points

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

What if there are several bottoms? What if the slope is 0 but it is not a bottom?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

global minimum
The lowest bottom of all.
local minimum
A bottom lower than its surroundings, but not the lowest.
saddle point
A bottom in one direction and a top in another, shaped like a saddle.

Two valleys

GD only looks at the ground under its feet, so where it starts decides which valley it ends in.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. Click anywhere on the curve: a ball rolls from there. The strip below shows which valley each starting point ends in.
  2. 20 random starts: some get trapped in the side valley.

Slope 0, but not a bottom

At (0, 0) it is a bottom along x and a top along y. Starting with y = 0, the slope along y is always 0, so GD stops here.

HOW TO READ THE FIGURE

  • A contour map: deeper blue is lower; lines join points of equal height. Orange = the path GD walked. Click the map to start there.

One line to remember. GD only sees what is under its feet. Depending on the start it can get trapped in a local minimum or linger near a saddle point.

8Two variables: the gradient vector

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

With several parameters, what is "the slope"? (Real models have millions.)

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

∂f/∂x
partial derivative. The slope when only x changes a little and everything else stays put.
∇f
"nabla f", the gradient. The vector of partial derivatives. It points straight uphill.
  • ∂f/∂x slope across
  • ∂f/∂y slope up and down
  • ∇f the two combined = uphill
  • −η∇f one step the other way

WORKED EXAMPLE: f = x² + 3y², one step from (2, 1) with η = 0.1

→ Meaning. The same rule as in 1D, applied to each variable. ∇f is perpendicular to the contour lines (it is the steepest direction).

THINGS TO TRY

  1. Click anywhere on the map: the red ∇f is always perpendicular to the contours, and the orange path heads the other way.
  2. Stretch k = 10: a narrow valley makes the path zigzag (normalization in chapter 6 and momentum in chapter 7 fix this).
  3. Ascent: follow the same arrow uphill (the loss grows).

One line to remember. The gradient is an arrow made of the slope along each variable. Walking against it updates every parameter at once.

9A first look at vanishing and exploding gradients

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

With 10 layers, the chain rule multiplies 10 stretch factors together. What if each factor is below 1, or above 1?

  • a one layer's factor
  • g₁ the gradient reaching layer 1
  • g_out the gradient at the output (= 1)

WORKED EXAMPLE

→ Meaning. 0.25 is the largest slope a sigmoid has. With 10 layers, layer 1 gets about a millionth of the gradient and barely learns (vanishing). With 1.5 it is 58 times too big (exploding). The fixes come in chapter 6 (ReLU, normalization) and chapter 9 (weight initialization).

One line to remember. In a deep network the gradient is a power of the per-layer factor. Stray a little from 1 and it vanishes or explodes.
