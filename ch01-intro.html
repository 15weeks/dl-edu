Foundations

The math, statistics and code behind deep learning

Deep learning is built from fewer tools than you might think: a way to line numbers up (vectors, matrices), a way to multiply and add them (the dot product), a way to turn scores into probabilities (softmax), a way to measure how wrong an answer is (cross-entropy), a way to tell whether a result can be trusted (mean, variance, the t-test), and a way to write all of this in a few lines (NumPy, PyTorch). This chapter takes those tools one at a time, computes each by hand, and lets you move it around in a picture.

1Turning the world into numbers: scalar, vector, matrix, tensor

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

Pictures, text and sound are not numbers. So how does a computer calculate with them? And what do numbers like (32, 3, 224, 224), which show up all over deep-learning code, actually mean?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

scalar
A single number. Example: a temperature of 21.5.
vector
Numbers lined up in one row. Example: [height, weight] = [172, 68].
matrix
Numbers laid out in a table of rows and columns. A greyscale photo is one.
tensor
The general name for numbers laid out along several directions (axes). A scalar has 0 axes, a vector 1, a matrix 2.
shape
How many numbers there are along each axis. Example: (28, 28) = 28 down, 28 across.
pixel
picture element. One dot of a picture. One brightness number in greyscale, three numbers (red, green, blue: RGB) in colour.
channel
The number of stacked number-sheets at the same position, such as R, G and B in a colour photo.
one-hot
A vector with a single 1 and 0 everywhere else. The simplest way to turn a category into numbers.

A deep-learning model only computes with numbers, so every piece of data is first turned into a bundle of numbers. The "form" of that bundle is its shape.

3Dot product: how much two vectors point the same way

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

What is the single most common calculation in deep learning? "Multiply matching entries and add them all up." What does that measure?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

a · b
dot product. Multiply the numbers in matching positions, then add everything up.
|a|
norm. The length of a vector: √(a₁² + a₂² + …).
θ
theta. The angle between two vectors.
w, b
weight, bias. The numbers a neuron multiplies its inputs x by, and the single number it adds.

Example ①: a = (2, 1), b = (1, 3)

Goal: multiply matching entries and add, then divide by the lengths to get the angle.

→ Meaning. The dot product measures how much two vectors point the same way. Same direction gives a large value, perpendicular gives 0, opposite gives a negative value. Dividing by the lengths (cos θ) compares direction only, regardless of length; face recognition uses exactly this, under the name cosine similarity.

Example ②: one neuron is one dot product

Goal: with input x = (1, 2, 3), weights w = (0.5, −1, 2) and bias b = 0.5, find the neuron's score z.

→ Meaning. A neuron uses the dot product to ask "how much does the input look like the pattern I like (w)?" The third input (3) times a large weight (2) makes most of the score. Learning means adjusting these w and b (chapters 2–3).

HOW TO READ THE FIGURE

  • Blue arrow = a, orange arrow = b. The dashed line drops b onto the direction of a; the thick strip it leaves is b's shadow (projection). Shadow length × |a| = a · b.

CONTROLS

Drag an arrow tip
Drag the end of a or b to change its direction and length.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. Start (a = (2, 1), b = (1, 3)): a · b = 5, angle 45° (example ①).
  2. Drag b until it is perpendicular to a: a · b becomes 0 and the shadow disappears.
  3. Drag b to the opposite side of a: a · b turns negative and the shadow appears on the far side of a.

One line to remember. Dot product = multiply matching entries and add = how much two vectors point the same way. One neuron computes exactly this (w · x + b).

4Matrix multiplication: many dot products at once

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

A layer with hundreds of neurons needs hundreds of dot products. Matrix multiplication writes them all at once. What is the rule, and how do the shapes have to fit?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

AB, A @ B
matrix multiplication. Written with @ in Python.
W
weight matrix. Each row holds the weights of one neuron.

One rule: cell (i, j) of the result = the dot product of row i of A and column j of B.

Shape rule: (m × n) @ (n × p) = (m × p). The two inner numbers (n) must match, because a dot product needs two vectors of the same length.

Example: one cell of the result, row 1, column 2

Goal: take the dot product of row 1 of A, (1, 2, 3), and column 2 of B, (0, 1, 1).

→ Meaning. The result has 2 × 2 = 4 cells, so this is 4 dot products. Seen as a neural-network layer, each row of W holds one neuron's weights, and a single matrix product W x computes every neuron of the layer at once. GPUs do exactly this kind of product very fast.

HOW TO READ THE FIGURE

  • A (2 × 3), B (3 × 2), result C = A @ B (2 × 2). Click a cell of the result to colour the row of A and column of B that made it.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. Click the top-right cell of the result (5): the box shows the example, 1 × 0 + 2 × 1 + 3 × 1 = 5.
  2. "Step through" visits the four cells one by one, showing one row of A meeting one column of B to make one cell of C.
  3. Press "New numbers", work out one cell by hand, then click it to check.
Click a cell of the result C.

One line to remember. Matrix multiplication = dot products arranged as a table. (m × n) @ (n × p) = (m × p); the inner sizes must match. One layer of neurons = one W x.

5Softmax: turning scores into probabilities

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

The last layer of a model gives one score per class, for example cat 2.0, dog 1.0, bird 0.0. How do we turn those scores into something like "66% likely to be a cat"?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

logit
The score z before softmax. It can be negative.
e
Euler's number, about 2.718. eᶻ grows 2.718 times each time z goes up by 1.
T
temperature. Divide the scores by T before softmax. Small T piles probability onto the winner; large T spreads it evenly.

Two steps. ① Turn every score into eᶻ, which is always positive. ② Divide by the total so everything adds up to 1.

Example: z = (2, 1, 0), T = 1

→ Meaning. A score gap of 1 becomes a probability ratio of about 2.7. With the same scores, T = 0.5 piles up on the winner, (0.867, 0.117, 0.016), and T = 2 spreads out, (0.506, 0.307, 0.186). The order (who wins) never depends on T.

HOW TO READ THE FIGURE

  • Left: the scores z (logits), positive above 0, negative below. Right: softmax probabilities, which always add up to 1. The largest probability (the argmax, section 8) is drawn solid.

CONTROLS

z₁, z₂, z₃
Scores for the three classes (cat, dog, bird).
T
Temperature.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. Add 5 to all three scores (for example 7, 6, 5): the probabilities do not change. Softmax only looks at the differences between scores.
  2. Set T to 0.2: almost all probability goes to the winner. Set T to 5: nearly 1/3 each.

One line to remember. Softmax = make every score positive with eᶻ, then divide by the total. The result is a set of probabilities that add to 1, and only the differences between scores matter. Temperature decides how strongly the winner takes all.

6Log and exp: tools that turn products into sums

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

Why does a log (ln) show up in almost every deep-learning loss?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

exp(x), eˣ
exponential. e multiplied by itself x times.
ln x
natural logarithm. "What power of e gives x?" The reverse of exp.
underflow
When a number gets so small that the computer treats it as 0.
  • ln undoes exp: ln(eˣ) = x. On a graph, each is the other flipped over the line y = x.
  • Product → sum: ln(a × b) = ln a + ln b. When many probabilities have to be multiplied, taking logs turns it into addition.
  • The ln of a probability (0 to 1) is 0 or below. The smaller the probability, the more negative its ln.

Example ①: a product becomes a sum

Example ②: multiplying a small probability 200 times

→ Meaning. A computer's float64 numbers become 0 below about 10⁻³⁰⁸. Multiplying probabilities directly hits 0 quickly, but with logs the answer stays an ordinary number such as −921. That is why deep learning works with log-probabilities instead of probabilities.

HOW TO READ THE FIGURE

  • Blue = y = ln x, orange = y = eˣ, grey dashed = y = x. The two dots are a mirrored pair across y = x.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. Set x to 1: ln 1 = 0. The log of probability 1 (certainty) is 0.
  2. Lower x to 0.05: ln x falls quickly below −3. The smaller the probability, the more negative its log.

One line to remember. ln is the reverse of exp, and it turns products into sums. Multiplied probabilities collapse to 0, so deep learning adds log-probabilities instead.

7Entropy and cross-entropy: average surprise

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

A model says "the answer is dog with probability 0.7". How wrong is that? Cross-entropy measures it with one number, and nearly every classification model uses it as its loss.

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

surprise
−ln p when something with probability p happens. The rarer the event, the larger the surprise.
H(p)
entropy. The average surprise: how hard the outcome is to predict.
CE
cross-entropy. The surprise between the true distribution y and the predicted distribution ŷ. When the answer is one-hot, it is −ln(probability given to the right answer).
nat, bit
Units of information. Measured with ln it is nats, with log₂ it is bits. 1 bit = 0.693 nat.

Example ①: the entropy of a coin

→ Meaning. A fair coin is the hardest to predict (maximum entropy). A coin that lands heads 99% of the time is almost never surprising (0.056).

Example ②: three or more outcomes

Goal: Σ adds over every possible outcome, so n outcomes give n terms. Writing one number, as in H(0.9), is shorthand that works only for two outcomes (knowing one fixes the other as 1 − p). With three or more, list every probability, as in H(0.5, 0.3, 0.2).

→ Meaning. With n outcomes, entropy is largest when all are equally likely (1/n), and that maximum is ln n: a coin ln 2 = 0.693, rock-paper-scissors ln 3 = 1.099, a die ln 6 = 1.792, ImageNet's 1000 classes ln 1000 = 6.908. (0.5, 0.3, 0.2) leans toward 0.5, so it is a little easier to predict and its entropy, 1.030, is below ln 3. In a classifier, softmax gives K class probabilities, so Σ has K terms (10 for MNIST). Cross-entropy's Σ also has K terms; with a one-hot answer only the right answer's term is left (example ③).

Example ③: two models' cross-entropy when the answer is "dog"

Goal: the answer is y = (0, 1, 0) (cat, dog, bird). Because it is one-hot, only −ln of the right answer's entry is left.

→ Meaning. Model A gives the right answer 0.7, so its loss is small; model B gives it only 0.3, so its loss is more than three times larger. Probability 1 on the right answer gives a loss of 0. Training adjusts the weights to push this number down. PyTorch's nn.CrossEntropyLoss does exactly this calculation (softmax + −ln).

HOW TO READ THE FIGURE

  • Left: the entropy H(p) of a coin against its probability of heads p. Highest in the middle (0.5).
  • Right: cross-entropy −ln q against the probability q that the model gave the right answer. As q approaches 0 the loss explodes.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. Set q to 0.7 (model A in the example): 0.357. Set q to 0.3 (model B): 1.204.
  2. Set q to 0.01: loss 4.6. Being confidently wrong is punished very hard.

One line to remember. Entropy = average surprise (−ln p). Cross-entropy = −ln of the probability the model gave the right answer. Classification models minimize it as their loss.

8Min/max and argmin/argmax: value and position

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

A model outputs probabilities (0.2, 0.7, 0.1). How is the final answer, "dog", chosen?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

max, min
The largest value, the smallest value.
argmax, argmin
argument of the maximum / minimum. The position (index) where the largest (smallest) value sits.
index
A position number. Python counts from 0.

Example: scores v = (2.0, 3.5, 1.2) (cat, dog, bird)

→ Meaning. max says "how much"; argmax says "where". In classification the answer is the argmax: index 1 (the second entry, counting from 0) = "dog". Softmax never changes the order, so the argmax of the scores equals the argmax of the probabilities; if you only need the answer you can skip softmax and take the argmax of the scores directly. Gradient descent in chapter 2 is the search for the argmin of the loss (the weights that make the loss smallest).

One line to remember. max = the largest value, argmax = where it sits. The answer of a classifier is an argmax; the goal of training is the argmin of the loss.

9Mean, variance, standard deviation: center and spread

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

You train a model five times and get a slightly different accuracy each time. How do you say "typically how much" and "how much it wobbles" with two numbers?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

x̄, μ
mean. x̄ = the mean of the data you have; μ = the true mean of the whole population.
variance, σ²
The average of the squared distances from the mean.
SD, σ
standard deviation. The square root of the variance: the "typical spread" in the data's own units.
n − 1
The correction of dividing by n − 1 instead of n when the data is a sample (sample variance).

Example: x = (2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9)

One entry: the first value, 2, is −3 away from the mean 5; squared, that is 9. Squaring keeps distances above and below the mean from cancelling each other out.

→ Meaning. This data is "typically 5, typically spread by about ±2". If the data is only part of the whole (a sample), divide by n − 1 instead: 32 ÷ 7 = 4.571, SD 2.138, slightly larger. NumPy's np.var divides by n by default; PyTorch's torch.var divides by n − 1 by default.

HOW TO READ THE FIGURE

  • Top: the 8 data points of the example (x₁ to x₈). The value 4 appears three times, so each point gets its own row to avoid overlap. Green vertical line = the mean; horizontal line from each point to the mean = its distance.
  • Bottom: each point's squared distance (x − mean)² as a bar. Green dashed line = the average bar height = the variance.

CONTROLS

Drag a point
Drag a point in the top panel left or right to change its value (steps of 0.5).
Reset values
Go back to the example data.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. Drag x₈ (9) to 10: its bar grows from 16 to about 24 and the variance rises from 4 to 5.11. Because of the squaring, one far-away value shakes the variance a lot.
  2. Gather all points in one place: every bar vanishes and the variance becomes 0.

One line to remember. Mean = the center. Variance = the average squared distance from the mean. SD = its square root (the typical spread, in the data's own units).

10Random sampling and seeds: reproducing chance

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

Deep learning uses randomness everywhere (initial weights, shuffling data, dropout), so results change from run to run. By how much, and how do you get the same result again?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

population, sample
The whole group, and the part of it you drew.
sampling variability
Numbers such as the mean change every time you draw a new sample.
SE
standard error. How much a sample mean wobbles = σ ÷ √n.
seed
The starting value of a random-number generator. Same seed, same "random" numbers.
→ Meaning. In a group whose test scores have mean 70 and SD 10, the mean of 10 people drawn at random typically wobbles by about ±3.2. With 100 people it shrinks to ±1. You need 100 times more people to cut the wobble to 1/10 (√100 = 10).
import numpy as np, torch
np.random.seed(42); print(np.random.rand(3))   # [0.3745 0.9507 0.732 ]
np.random.seed(42); print(np.random.rand(3))   # the same again: [0.3745 0.9507 0.732 ]
torch.manual_seed(42)                          # PyTorch has its own seed

HOW TO READ THE FIGURE

  • Left, grey = the population: how test scores (mean 70, SD 10) are spread.
  • Right, blue = a histogram of the means of many samples of n people. Red curve = the shape theory predicts (centered at 70, width SE).

CONTROLS

n
How many people go into one sample.
Draw buttons
Draw one sample, or 1,000, and pile up their means.
seed
Draw one sample with this seed. The same seed always gives the same sample.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. n = 10, draw 1,000: the means pile up around 70 ± 3. With n = 100 the pile is about a third as wide.
  2. Draw twice with seed 42: identical numbers. Change the seed to 43 and they differ. This page's generator is not NumPy's, so seed 42 here does not give NumPy's numbers.
n

One line to remember. Random results wobble from draw to draw, and a mean wobbles by σ ÷ √n. To get the same result again, fix the seed.

11The t-test: is the difference real?

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

Model A averaged 0.82 accuracy and model B 0.85, five training runs each. Is B really better, or is it luck?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

t
t statistic. The difference in means ÷ the wobble of that difference (SE). "Signal ÷ noise".
df
degrees of freedom. A number set by the amount of data; it decides the shape of the t distribution.
p-value
The chance of getting a t this large by luck alone if the true difference were 0. Below 0.05 is usually read as "hard to explain by chance".
Welch
Named after Bernard L. Welch. A t-test that works even when the two groups have different spreads.

Example: A = (0.81, 0.83, 0.80, 0.82, 0.84), B = (0.84, 0.86, 0.83, 0.85, 0.87)

① Means: A 0.82, B 0.85, difference 0.03. ② Each group's SD (dividing by n − 1) is 0.0158.

→ Meaning. The difference (0.03) is three times its wobble (0.01), which is hard to explain by luck (p = 0.017 < 0.05). The same 0.03 difference with a wobble of 0.03 would give t = 1 and could easily be luck. That is why models are compared over several runs with different seeds, looking at both the mean and the wobble.

HOW TO READ THE FIGURE

  • Orange dots = the accuracy of each run of model A, blue dots = model B. Thick vertical line = the mean, horizontal bar = mean ± SE.

CONTROLS

True difference
How much better B really is (this is a simulation, so you can set it).
Noise SD
How much each run wobbles.
n
Runs per model (number of seeds).
Redraw
Run the experiment again with the same settings. The starting screen shows the example's numbers.
Repeat 200×
Run 200 new experiments with the current settings and count how often p < 0.05.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. True difference 0.03, noise 0.016, n = 5, redraw a few times: t is usually around 2–4 and p is often below 0.05. Repeating 200 times gives p < 0.05 about 70% of the time.
  2. Raise the noise to 0.04: the same difference now often gives p above 0.05 (only about 16% of 200 repeats are below). With a large wobble the difference is hard to trust.
  3. Put the noise back to 0.016 and set n to 10: about 98% give p < 0.05. More runs catch a real difference more reliably.
  4. Set the true difference to 0 and repeat 200 times: about 5% (1 in 20) still give p < 0.05. That is being "significant by chance" when there is no difference at all.

One line to remember. t = difference in means ÷ the wobble of that difference. When the signal is well above the noise (p < 0.05), luck is a poor explanation. Compare models over several seeds.

12First steps in Python, NumPy and PyTorch

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

How are the calculations above written in code? What do NumPy and PyTorch have in common, and how do they differ?

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

NumPy
Numerical Python. A library for calculating with arrays of numbers. Runs on the CPU.
PyTorch
A deep-learning library. Almost the same tensor calculations as NumPy, plus GPUs and automatic differentiation (autograd).
GPU
Graphics Processing Unit. Hardware that runs thousands of identical calculations at the same time.
autograd
automatic differentiation. Records the steps of a calculation and computes the derivatives (gradients) automatically.
broadcasting
The rule that stretches size-1 axes so that arrays of different shapes can be combined.
Colab
Google Colaboratory. A browser notebook that runs Python, and a GPU, with nothing to install.
  • A Python list is not built for arithmetic: [1, 2, 3] * 2 = [1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3] (repetition). A NumPy array does arithmetic: np.array([1, 2, 3]) * 2 = [2, 4, 6].
  • A PyTorch tensor is used almost exactly like a NumPy array, with two extras: ① .to("cuda") moves it to the GPU; ② requires_grad=True makes PyTorch compute derivatives automatically.
  • Starting in Google Colab means nothing to install.
# NumPy: this chapter's calculations
import numpy as np
A = np.array([[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]])
B = np.array([[1, 0], [0, 1], [1, 1]])
print(A.shape, A.T.shape)        # (2, 3) (3, 2)
print(np.array([2, 1]) @ np.array([1, 3]))   # 5
print(A @ B)                     # [[ 4  5] [10 11]]

z = np.array([2.0, 1.0, 0.0])
p = np.exp(z) / np.exp(z).sum()  # softmax
print(p.round(3))                # [0.665 0.245 0.09 ]
print(-np.log(0.7))              # 0.357 (cross-entropy)

print(x.mean(), x.var(), x.std())  # 5.0 4.0 2.0
# PyTorch: the same, plus GPU and autograd

A = torch.tensor([[1., 2., 3.], [4., 5., 6.]])
B = torch.tensor([[1., 0.], [0., 1.], [1., 1.]])
print(A.shape)                   # torch.Size([2, 3])
print((A @ B).tolist())          # [[4.0, 5.0], [10.0, 11.0]]
print(torch.softmax(torch.tensor([2., 1., 0.]), dim=0))
# tensor([0.6652, 0.2447, 0.0900])

y = x ** 2
y.backward()                     # computes dy/dx automatically
print(x.grad)                    # tensor(6.)

13A first taste of derivatives: the slope is "how much it changes"

WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW

Learning means nudging the weights in the direction that lowers the loss. How do we know which direction, and by how much? The derivative (slope) is the answer. Chapter 2 covers it properly; here we just get a feel for it.

TERMS IN THIS SECTION

dy/dx, f′(x)
derivative. How fast y changes when x changes by a tiny amount = the slope at that point.
h
The small amount by which x is changed.
gradient
When there are several variables, the vector that collects all their derivatives.

Example: the slope of f(x) = x² at x = 3

→ Meaning. At x = 3, nudging x up makes y grow about 6 times as much. The slope is positive, so to make y smaller we must make x smaller. That is all gradient descent (chapter 2) is: "take a small step against the slope". The x.grad = 6 that PyTorch computed automatically in section 12 is this same number.

HOW TO READ THE FIGURE

  • Curve = y = x². Green line = the tangent at x (slope 2x). Orange line = the secant through x and x + h. As h shrinks, the orange line snaps onto the green one.

THINGS TO TRY

  1. x = 3, h = 2: secant slope 8, far from the true 6. Shrink h to 0.01 and it becomes 6.01.
  2. Set x to 0: slope 0. The bottom of the curve, the minimum of the loss (its argmin), is where the slope is 0.

One line to remember. Derivative = the rate of change under a tiny nudge = the slope. Stepping against the slope makes the value smaller. Training does this for millions of weights at once.
